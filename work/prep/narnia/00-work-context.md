# 00 — Work Context: The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe

## Author & era
- **Author:** Clive Staples ("C.S.") Lewis (1898–1963) — British/Irish-born scholar of medieval and Renaissance literature at Oxford (and later Cambridge), Christian apologist, and member of the Church of England. (Britannica; Wikipedia, *Chronicles of Narnia*.)
- **Written/published:** *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* (LWW) was published **16 October 1950** by Geoffrey Bles, as the first-published of seven Chronicles; the series concluded with *The Last Battle* in 1956. (Britannica; Wikipedia, *The Chronicles of Narnia*.)
- **The Inklings:** Lewis was a core member of the Inklings, an informal Oxford literary discussion group (active late 1930s–mid-1940s) whose nucleus was **Lewis and J.R.R. Tolkien**, with Charles Williams, Owen Barfield, Warren Lewis, Hugo Dyson and others. Works read aloud to the group included Tolkien's *Lord of the Rings* and Lewis's *Out of the Silent Planet* / *Perelandra*. LWW itself was reportedly met with skepticism by Tolkien in early drafts. (America Magazine; Ignatius/loc; Tea & Ink Society.)
- **WWII evacuation backdrop:** During WWII, London children were evacuated from the Blitz and Lewis housed evacuees at his Oxford home, The Kilns. Scholars widely speculate that their presence seeded the premise of the Pevensie siblings — Peter, Susan, Edmund and Lucy — sent from London to a remote professor's country house. (Sharon Wray; this origin is the commonly cited biographical frame, marked as "speculated" in scholarship.)

## Genre & form
- **Genre:** Children's fantasy; specifically **portal fantasy** (the wardrobe is the threshold from wartime England into Narnia). The Chronicles as a whole are characterized as a series of "portal fantasy novels." (Wikipedia, *The Chronicles of Narnia*; Britannica.)
- **Form:** A novel for children; the series was **illustrated by Pauline Baynes**, whose illustrations are the canonical visual interpretation. (Wikipedia, *The Chronicles of Narnia*.)
- **Structure:** LWW is conventionally cited as **17 chapters**. (Standard edition pagination; corroborated by the local text-track copy.)
- **Subtitle note:** Lewis used the subtitle *"A Story for Children"* for only two of the seven Chronicles — LWW and *The Last Battle* — functioning as an "inclusio" around the series. (The Gospel Coalition / Trevin Wax.)

## The allegory question (load-bearing for adaptation)
This is the single most contested interpretive axis for the work, and the adaptation's **allegory-stance** decision must account for BOTH framings.

- **The common allegorical reading:** LWW is widely read as a **Christian allegory** — Aslan as a Christ figure; his voluntary sacrifice at the Stone Table and subsequent resurrection mapping onto the crucifixion and resurrection; Edmund's betrayal (treachery for Turkish Delight) and redemption standing in for sin and grace; the White Witch / Jadis as a Satanic or death figure; and the legal-magic framework of the **"Deep Magic"** (law requiring traitor's blood) and the **"Deeper Magic from before the Dawn of Time"** (which reverses death when a willing innocent is sacrificed) echoing Law and Gospel. Britannica and most popular criticism describe the work in these terms; Christian teaching resources (e.g., C.S. Lewis Institute, Ditchfield) explicitly enumerate the biblical parallels. (Britannica; CS Lewis Institute; Christian Century.)

- **Lewis's own framing — "supposal," NOT allegory:** Lewis, a professional literary scholar who wrote the academic monograph *The Allegory of Love*, repeatedly denied that LWW is an allegory. His own statement (as preserved in correspondence and quoted in criticism):
  > "If Aslan represented the immaterial Deity in the same way in which Giant Despair represents Despair, he would be an allegorical figure. In reality however he is an invention giving an imaginary answer to the question, 'What might Christ become like if there really were a world like Narnia and He chose to be incarnate and die and rise again in that world as He actually has done in ours?' This is not an allegory at all... This also works out a supposition."
  He distinguished strict allegory (where a secondary figure *stands in for* a hidden primary subject) from what he called a **"supposal"** — a counterfactual imaginative exercise in which Christ is *literally incarnated* in that world as Aslan. Lewis elsewhere: "Narnia is Christian. People think Aslan is just a symbol for Jesus... No, he IS Jesus" (in that world). (NarniaWeb Forum — "Allegorical Aslan – C.S. Lewis Quote"; A Pilgrim in Narnia, via Gopnik/New Yorker; The Gospel Coalition; stgb Substack.)

  > **Implication for adaptation:** The text track should treat Aslan's actions as narratively real *within Narnia*, not as one-to-one cipher mappings, while the context track records that most readers (and Lewis's critics) perceive an allegorical structure regardless of the author's denial. The "supposal" framing is the stronger authorial-intent anchor; the allegorical reading is the stronger reception-history anchor. Both are defensible and must be surfaced to the operator.

## Reception & audience
- **Status:** A classic of children's literature and one of the best-selling children's series of all time; a staple of ages ~8–12 reading. (Britannica; Wikipedia.)
- **Initial reception:** LWW "received mixed reviews and didn't sell as well as the publisher expected" at first, but its "charm, rich symbolism, and captivating world eventually won over readers." (Sharon Wray.)
- **Religious-content controversy:** The Christian dimension has been a sustained flashpoint. **Philip Pullman** (*His Dark Materials*) has been an outspoken critic, calling LWW's religious content proselytizing and singling out themes he considered morally objectionable (e.g., the "filthy lie" that Susan is excluded from Narnia for growing interested in "nylons and lipstick and invitations"). Pullman has framed his own *His Dark Materials* in part as an atheist "riposte" to Narnia. (Christian Century; Tony Watkins; Russell F. Hirsch / Storythreads.)
- **Reading-order controversy (context):** Though LWW was first-published, HarperCollins renumbered the series to internal-chronological order (placing *The Magician's Nephew* first) on the grounds of Lewis's stated preference; scholars and fans widely argue publication order (LWW first) is the stronger introduction. (NarniaWeb; Reactor; Stack Exchange.)

## Prior adaptations (context only)
- **1988 BBC television serial** (adapted 1988–1990 across the Pevensie books) — animated/limited-effects children's TV; noted as a faithful but budget-limited version. (Frame Rated; NarniaWeb.)
- **2005 film** — *The Chronicles of Narnia: The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*, directed by Andrew Adamson, produced by **Walden Media** and distributed by **Walt Disney Pictures**; premiered 7 Dec 2005 (Royal Film Performance), wide release 8–9 Dec 2005. The highest-profile screen adaptation to date. (Wikipedia film entry; Narnia Fandom; IMDb.)
- **Stage** — multiple theatrical/stage adaptations exist (noted in secondary commentary). These are adaptation precedent, not source material for the text track. (Frame Rated.)

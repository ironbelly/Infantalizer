# Reader-Sim Report — Ch.01 Tier 1 ("Lucy Finds the Snowy Land")

**Reader persona:** Preschool, ages 4–6. Read-aloud. Preoperational, animistic,
egocentric. Emotion vocabulary {happy, sad, scared, grumpy, tired, excited, brave,
kind}. Needs safety and warmth; wonders at concrete sensory magic.
**Work / chapter:** Narnia, Chapter 1. **Draft:** `ch-01-t1-v2.md`.
**This is the child's FIRST chapter of the whole book.**

This is the experiential report of how the chapter *landed* for this specific child
— not a craft diagnosis. Felt experience is reported even where the child cannot
articulate the cause.

---

## Emotional arc, in order (what the child felt, beat by beat)

1. **Settled / safe.** Four children, names said one at a time, a big house, a kind
   old Professor with wild white hair, Mrs. Macready giving "a biscuit and a
   smile." *Lucy felt safe right away.* The child feels safe too. The biscuit is
   doing real work here — it is a concrete, bodily proof of kindness a 4-year-old
   can hold.
2. **A little wobble, fast fixed.** Edmund is "grumpy and tired." A small cloud on
   the page. But "next day he felt bright again" resolves it inside one sentence.
   The child does not sit with the grumpy long enough to absorb it as mood. Good.
3. **Cozy exploring.** Pictures, a shiny suit of armour, an empty room. The child
   leans in a little — "what's in the empty room?" Curiosity reward is engaged.
4. **The cupboard.** Tall and dark. *Mild alert.* "Tall and dark" is the first
   thing in the chapter that isn't safe. The child notices but is not scared,
   because Lucy is the one acting and Lucy was just declared safe. A 5-year-old
   will read their own safety onto Lucy.
5. **The threshold — peak wonder.** The door creaks, the coats are soft and furry
   and smell of fur and mothballs, Lucy rubs her face on them, and then — *crunch,
   crunch.* Snow under her feet. Branches on her face. A light ahead. **This is the
   strongest lean-in moment of the chapter.** The sensory list is exactly what a
   4–6yo can grip: soft, furry, smell, crunch, cold, white, rough, light. The
   child is leaning forward here. They want the page turned.
6. **Into the wood.** Snow on her nose, snow on her hair. Trees dark and tall, but
   the snow makes it bright. *She could still see the cupboard door. She could go
   home.* **This is the safety rope.** The child registers it: she is NOT trapped.
   This is the move that lets the child enjoy the wood instead of fearing it.
7. **The lamp-post — second peak wonder.** "Lucy had never seen a lamp-post in a
   wood before. How funny!" The child laughs. A lamp-post in a wood is exactly the
   kind of concrete, slightly-silly magic a preschooler finds delightful. They are
   fully with Lucy here.
8. **The approaching sound — alert, not fear.** *Pit-pat, pit-pat.* Someone coming.
   A small frisson. The child is alert but not scared because of the warm lamp-post
   and the safe feeling carried from the cupboard.
9. **The Faun — delight.** A little person, Lucy's size. Curly hair, red scarf,
   hoofs like a goat, an umbrella, brown paper parcels. "He looked ready for a
   party." The child loves this. It is a friendly, silly, party-shaped person.
10. **The startle — resolved fast.** The Faun jumps and drops his parcels: *bump,
    bump, bump.* Lucy laughs. The Faun laughs. "He had a kind face and a warm
    smile." Any fright from the jump collapses into shared laughter inside one
    sentence. The child is giggling, not frightened.
11. **Warm landing.** "Hello, Lucy," said the kind Faun. Smile. **The child ends
    safe, warm, and curious** — who is this funny goat-person? What is the snowy
    land? They want the next chapter.

The arc: **safe → small wobble (fixed) → cozy curiosity → wonder (portal) → safe
wonder (wood) → delight (lamp-post) → small startle (fixed by laughter) → warm
landing.** It resolves positively. It never leaves the child frightened.

---

## Where the child leans in / wants the page turned

- **The cupboard threshold (peak).** *Crunch, crunch. Snow.* The move from coats
  to snow is the chapter's magic moment. A 4–6yo will physically lean forward and
  may say "snow!" out loud. This is the strongest lean-in.
- **The lamp-post.** "A lamp-post in a wood! How funny!" A preschooler finds this
  genuinely delightful and slightly absurd in exactly the register they enjoy.
  Second strongest lean-in.
- **The Faun arriving with parcels.** "He looked ready for a party." Hoofs like a
  goat, parcels bumping in the snow. The child is delighted and curious — they
  want to know who he is. Third lean-in.

All three are concrete, sensory, and warm. None require abstract motive. Exactly
the reward channel a preoperational child can run on: **sensory transportation +
curiosity through concrete novelty**, not social simulation of hidden motives.

---

## Where the child could feel unsafe / scared (and whether it resolves)

- **"Tall and dark" cupboard.** A tiny flag. Resolved immediately because Lucy
  enters and is not frightened. No lingering dread. *Fine.*
- **The wood at night, dark trees.** A real risk for a 4-year-old at bedtime. The
  draft handles it correctly: *the snow made it bright* (darkness fixed) and *she
  could still see the cupboard door / she could go home* (escape visible). This
  is the tier-1 safety rope done right. Resolves.
- **The Faun's startle / dropping parcels.** The sudden "He jumped!" could tip a
  sensitive 5-year-old toward fright. Resolved in the same beat: Lucy laughs, the
  parcels go *bump bump bump* (silly, not scary), the Faun laughs, he is given a
  "kind face and a warm smile." The resolution is faster than the startle had time
  to build. Resolves well.
- **No unresolved scares.** Every alert in the chapter is closed inside one or two
  beats. This matches the tier-1 `requires_positive_resolution: true` and the
  `safe_home_schema` requirement (Lucy can see the door home throughout).

One note for the adult reader: the word "mothballs" will be unfamiliar to most
4–6yos. It will not scare them — it will just slide past as a smell-word they don't
have a referent for. Not a safety issue. Possibly a "what's a mothball?" question
at read-aloud time, which is a feature, not a bug.

---

## Where the child loses the thread / gets bored / doesn't understand

- **Paragraph 1 front-loads four names and two adults.** "Peter, Susan, Edmund,
  and Lucy" + the Professor + Mrs. Macready. A preschooler cannot hold six proper
  nouns in one breath. They will lock onto Lucy (the one who gets the biscuit and
  the smile) and let the others blur. This is actually fine for this chapter —
  Lucy is the POV — but the child is not tracking "the four children" as a set.
  Worth knowing for later chapters that need the siblings distinguished.
- **"Mrs. Macready kept the house tidy."** What does "kept the house tidy" mean
  to a 4-year-old? Very little. She is absorbed only through the biscuit and
  smile. Acceptable — the sentence does its work for older listeners without
  blocking the younger ones.
- **No loss-of-thread in the middle.** The cupboard → snow → wood → lamp-post →
  Faun sequence is a clean linear chain with no abstraction, no flashback, no
  motive to hold. This is precisely the `strictly_linear` and
  `max_clauses: 1` targets from the tier-1 profile, met. Flow reward is strong
  here for this reader.
- **No sentence exceeded the child's hold.** No long compound constructions. The
  sensory beats are delivered in short declarative chunks that match a read-aloud
  cadence. Aesthetic channel is working at the rhythm level even where the
  vocabulary is plain.

---

## Does the ending leave the child in a good emotional place?

**Yes.** Safe, warm, curious-to-continue. The last image is a kind Faun smiling
at Lucy by name. The child is not carrying any unresolved fear, any unanswered
scary question, or any image that could trouble bedtime. They are carrying a
friendly goat-man, a lamp-post in the snow, and the feeling that the cupboard is a
door to somewhere good.

The ending is also genuinely *curious* without being *suspenseful* — a hard
balance at tier 1. It opens a question ("who is the Faun? what is this land?")
without withholding safety. That is the right way to pull a preschooler into
Chapter 2.

## Knowledge boundary — what does the child learn/assume going forward?

- **The Faun is a FRIEND.** The child leaves Chapter 1 certain the Faun is kind
  ("kind face," "warm smile," he laughs with her, he says her name gently). This
  is the heaviest assumption the chapter creates. In the source, Mr Tumnus is
  grooming Lucy for the Witch at this exact point — his warmth is the bait. Tier
  1 cannot carry that ambiguity, so the draft commits fully to "the Faun is
  kind." Later chapters (2 and the rescue) will need to either (a) keep Tumnus
  genuinely friendly throughout (cooperation reframing — Tumnus helps Lucy escape
  because he chooses friendship), or (b) introduce a *different* source of danger
  so Tumnus's warmth can stay intact. The child has been promised this Faun is
  safe. Do not break that promise.
- **Narnia is a safe, magical place to visit.** The wood is bright, the lamp is
  warm, the snow is fun. There is no hint of the Witch yet. When the Witch
  appears in later chapters, the draft will need to do work to keep her within
  the tier-1 "grumpy/sad/confused, not evil" reframe without contradicting the
  brightness established here. The brightness is not a bug — it is the safety
  floor — but it is an obligation for what comes next.
- **The cupboard is a way home and a way back.** The child knows Lucy can return
  (the door is visible). This is the safety valve for the whole Narnia arc at
  tier 1. Later chapters can lean on it whenever the child needs reassurance that
  Lucy is not trapped.
- **Edmund = grumpy-but-fixable.** The child has a small, early label for Edmund:
  he was grumpy and then he wasn't. This will matter when Edmund's later arc
  arrives at this tier. The "grumpy is a state, not a nature" framing is already
  seeded correctly per `agency_externalization: mandatory`.
- **Lucy is brave and curious.** The child identifies Lucy as the one who stays
  behind, steps in, walks toward the light. They have a model of "brave" that is
  grounded in concrete action, not label. Good for later chapters where Lucy's
  choices drive the plot.

No assumption the child makes here is wrong within tier 1. Several of them are
*load-bearing* for downstream chapters and should be flagged to the chronicler:
the Faun-is-friend commitment is the biggest one.

## Reward-channel read for this reader

- **Transportation:** Strong. Concrete sensory chain (fur, crunch, snow, lamp,
  parcels). The child is *in* the wood.
- **Aesthetic:** Working at rhythm level. Short beats, onomatopoeia the child
  will echo (*crunch crunch*, *bump bump bump*). Plain vocabulary but musical.
- **Social simulation:** Light — appropriate for tier 1. The child models Lucy
  (safe, curious, brave) and the Faun (kind, funny). No hidden motive is asked
  of them, which is correct for preoperational cognition.
- **Curiosity / prediction:** Engaged and healthy. "What's in the cupboard? What
  is the light? Who is coming?" All opened and either answered or carried forward
  at the right pace. The ending's open question is the pull into Ch.2.
- **Flow:** Smooth. No sentence stops the child. Linear, no flashbacks, no
  abstraction. This is the channel the draft most clearly protects.

---

## Verdict (this reader)

**Yes.** The chapter lands for a 4–6-year-old being read to. It opens safe,
crosses a wonder-threshold, never leaves the child frightened, and ends warm and
curious. The single most important downstream commitment it creates — the Faun is
a friend — is the right commitment for tier 1, and the chronicler should be told
it is now in the knowledge boundary.

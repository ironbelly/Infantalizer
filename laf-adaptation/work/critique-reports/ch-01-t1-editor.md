## 1. Overall diagnosis

Structurally sound, brief-compliant, and safe; its dominant weakness is a recurring habit of
**labeling emotions where its own sensory voice has already done the work**. It needs one focused
line/voice revision pass, not restructuring.

The architecture is right: the promise (gentle evacuation → big house → wardrobe → wood → Faun)
is set and paid; the wardrobe threshold (paras 5–6) is the draft's strongest passage and is
exactly the wonder beat the chapter exists to deliver; the cliffhanger is resolved into friendship
within the chapter per the greenlit T1 decision; and the safety anchors (Lucy can still see the
cupboard door; the Faun is immediately a friend) are present and correctly placed. Sentence length
is fully compliant (≤12 words) and there are zero em-dashes. Word count is well under the 800 cap.

What holds it back is voice-level: in the connective and emotional beats the prose swaps out of its
strong sensory register into label-mode ("Lucy felt excited. She felt brave, too."), and that swap is
the single biggest reader-cost in the draft. A pass that thins those labels and lets the sensory
voice (which the author clearly has — see "Crunch, crunch," the fur-and-mothballs bridge, "bump,
bump, bump") carry the feeling instead will lift the aesthetic and social-simulation channels
without touching structure.

**Verdict on this v1:** revision pass needed (line/voice, not structural). See §6.

## 3. Developmental notes

### 3.1 Promise, arc, causality — sound; no structural surgery

The reader promise is correctly set and paid: gentle wartime evacuation → mysterious house →
wardrobe threshold → winter wood → friendly Faun → safe, positive close. This is the portal/wonder
beat the analysis (`ch-01.yaml → meaning`) names as the chapter's job, and the draft delivers it.
The Faun-as-friend resolution (paras 13–15) correctly converts the source's startled-cliffhanger
closing line ("Goodness gracious me!") into warmth *within the chapter*, which is the explicit T1
greenlit move. **Do not change this ending** — it is protected intent working as designed.

### 3.2 Pacing balance — query, do not direct

Proportion check: opening/house setup ~4 paragraphs; wardrobe threshold (the chapter's load-bearing
wonder beat) ~2; snowy wood + lamp-post ~3; Faun arrival + resolution ~6.

The wonder beat is compressed relative to the Faun resolution. **But** the brief explicitly
prioritizes "end safe and positive" and "cliffhanger resolved into friendship," so the heavy Faun
real estate is greenlit-intentional, not drift — the friendship *is* the safety guarantee for a
4–6-year-old. **Query to author:** is one more sensory beat inside the wardrobe crossing
(see 3.3) the right trade, or does adding there slow the arrival at the safety resolution you
deliberately front-loaded? I lean toward adding it; you may not. Flagging, not prescribing.

### 3.3 Causality gap at the para 7→8 seam (the one real developmental note)

Para 7 lands the safety anchor ("She could still see the cupboard door. She could go home.") —
correct and necessary. Para 8 then jumps to "Lucy felt excited. She felt brave, too. She began to
walk." There is no *reason* given for Lucy to walk deeper into a night wood away from a door she
can still see. The source motivates this with curiosity about the light; the draft drops the
motivation and substitutes two emotion labels.

This is the highest-leverage developmental note because fixing it also fixes the dominant line-level
problem: replace the labels with one behavioral motivation beat (Lucy notices the light is warm /
pretty / inviting and *wants* to see it), and you get causality *and* sensory voice in the same
move. **Suggested direction only** — e.g., a beat that lets the light's warmth pull Lucy forward,
not a sentence that says "she was curious." Trust the image.

### 3.4 "Kind" — query whether this is a motif

"A kind old man" / "a kind woman" / "a kind face and a warm smile" / "the kind Faun." Four uses of
"kind" across ~15 paragraphs. For a picture book this *could* be a deliberate through-line word
(the chapter's claim: the world Lucy is entering is full of kind people, including the strange
one). If so, it needs to be earned by *varied proof* at each appearance, not by repeating the
adjective — right now the proof at the Faun ("he laughed, his parcels looked funny") is strong but
the proof at the Professor and Macready is absent (we're simply told they're kind). **Query:** is
"kind" an intentional spine-word for this chapter? If yes, vary the evidence; if no, thin to 1–2
uses so the word keeps its weight.

## 4. Line / voice notes

### 4.1 Pattern: emotion-label-as-beat (dominant line-level pattern)

The text does the work, then a sentence explains what the work accomplished — the canonical
LLM-writing redundancy failure (`writing-principles`: "the text does the work, then a sentence
explains what the work accomplished"). The sensory/behavioral image is almost always stronger than
the label that follows it.

Representative examples:

- **Para 8:** "Lucy felt excited. She felt brave, too." Two pure labels, zero behavior, stacked.
  The behavior is already there ("She began to walk. Crunch, crunch, crunch.") — the labels are
  redundant cargo. *Direction:* cut or convert to one anchor; let the walking and the crunching
  carry the feeling. (T1 explicitly permits naming "excited/brave," so one anchor label is fine —
  the issue is the stack, not the existence.)
- **Para 13:** "He had a kind face and a warm smile. He was a friend." The laugh ("The Faun
  laughed, too") already established friendship; three labels in two sentences ("kind face,"
  "warm smile," "He was a friend") over-explain it. *Direction:* keep one image (the smile is the
  most concrete), let "He was a friend" be implied by the laugh and the smile rather than stated.
- **Para 1:** "Lucy liked them both at once." Pure conclusion with no evidence on the page. For an
  opening paragraph this is the most costly instance because it's where the voice promise is set.
  *Direction:* one tiny behavioral proof, or cut.

Reader cost: aesthetic (the prose goes flat at exactly the moments that should sing) and
social-simulation (the child-listener is handed the conclusion instead of getting to feel it). This
is the pattern to hunt across the whole draft in v2.

### 4.2 Pattern: sentence-shape monotony in the opening

Para 1 is seven short declaratives in near-identical shape (subject-verb or subject-verb-object),
broken only weakly by "His name was the Professor." The draft *elsewhere* deploys exactly the tools
that would vary this — the question ("Where was the back of the cupboard?"), the exclamation
fragment ("Snow!"), the onomatopoeia ("Crunch, crunch."), the observational
aside ("How funny!"). None of those tools appear in para 1. The opening reads like a catalog
being checked off; the rest of the draft reads like a story. *Direction:* let para 1 borrow one or
two of the shapes the draft already uses well. Not all seven sentences need to be declarative.

### 4.3 Pattern: double-telling / opened-opened

- **Para 5:** "She opened the cupboard door. It opened right up." The second clause restates the
  first and adds nothing. Either compress to one beat, or make the second sentence carry new
  information — the *ease* of it, or the small surprise. (Note: the source has the door dropping
  two moth-balls on opening — a concrete beat that got abstracted away into "smelled of fur and
  mothballs" two sentences later. Consider whether restoring the dropped-mothballs moment earns the
  second sentence. Query — this is a source-detail choice, not an error.)

### 4.4 What to protect (the draft's strengths)

- **The onomatopoeia** — "Crunch, crunch," "Pit-pat, pit-pat," "bump, bump, bump." This is the
  draft's best ear and it is doing the picture-book work that the emotion-labels are failing to do.
  Do not thin these in v2; lean into them.
- **The sensory bridge of the threshold crossing (paras 5–6).** Fur → mothballs → face-rub →
  deeper → arms out → crunch → branches → light. Concrete, sequential, no over-explanation. This
  is the section that most rewards the reader. Preserve its discipline.
- **The safety anchor at para 7.** "She could still see the cupboard door. She could go home." This
  is the T1 `safe_home_schema` requirement (`tier_1.yaml → safety`) executed exactly right — the
  child reader knows Lucy is not trapped. Keep.
- **Zero em-dashes.** Clean on the punctuation-tell front.
- **POV discipline.** Consistently close-third on Lucy throughout, with exactly one narrator-verdict
  slip ("He was a friend," §4.1) — minor and fixable in the same pass.

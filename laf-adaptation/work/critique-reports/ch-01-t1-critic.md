## Significant

### S1 — The Faun description is six "He..." sentences in a row; the read-aloud turns into a catalog

Anchor (draft paragraph beginning line 21):

> "He was only as tall as Lucy. He had curly hair and a red scarf. He had
> little hoofs like a goat. He held a big umbrella up high. He carried
> brown paper parcels. He looked ready for a party."

Six sentences, five of them starting "He [verb]…", five with identical
" adjective object" shape. This is the chapter's most repetitive stretch.
Spoken aloud, the subject pronoun lands like a drum-beat at the front of
every clause and the reader's ear flattens — at the exact moment the Faun
is *first seen* (the chapter's single biggest reveal). The reveal lands as
a list of attributes rather than a creature stepping into the snow.

**Reader cost:** Aesthetic channel — the most-protected channel for this
register — goes monotone at the moment the chapter owes the reader a jolt
of wonder. This is the one beat a 4–6-year-old will sit up for, and the
prose doesn't sit up with them.

**Fix direction:** Vary the openings. Let one sentence lead with the
object ("A red scarf was wound round his neck"), let one be a question or
exclamation a child reader would think ("And look — he had little hoofs,
just like a goat!"), keep one short declarative. Three or four shapes
instead of six identical ones.

### S2 — The wonder beat (wardrobe → snowy wood) is told, not felt; the threshold crossing under-delivers

Anchor (draft lines 13–15):

> "Then she felt something new. Crunch, crunch. Something soft was under her
> feet. It was cold and white. Snow! Something rough brushed her face. It
> felt like branches. A light shone far ahead."

This is the chapter's load-bearing moment. Per `ch-01.yaml` meaning:
*"the mundane English wartime setting is deliberately ruptured by
wonder."* Here the rupture is delivered as a sequence of equivalent
declaratives — crunch, something soft, cold and white, snow, something
rough, branches — each roughly the same length and weight, so nothing
reads as the *turn*. "Snow!" is the single elevated beat and it does land,
but it's immediately followed by another flat "Something rough brushed her
face" that resets the level back to baseline.

**Reader cost:** Wonder is the stated reward channel for this register, and
the portal beat is the chapter's reason for existing. A child being read
to should gasp at the threshold; here the rhythm doesn't change when the
world does, so the gasp is smaller than the moment deserves. Compare the
source's own compression — soft powdery cold underfoot, rough prickly
branches, all in one breath — which does more wonder-work in fewer moves by
letting the senses collide rather than queuing them one per sentence.

**Fix direction:** Collapse two sensory beats into one image at the moment
of crossing ("Her feet felt something soft and cold. Snow!"). Let sentence
*shape* change at the turn — a fragment, a question, a longer rush — so
the reader's ear hears the world change even before the words register.

### S3 — The opening is nine subject-first declaratives before anything moves; the read-aloud takes too long to find its pulse

Anchor (draft line 3):

> "Once there were four children. Their names were Peter, Susan, Edmund, and
> Lucy. They went to live in a big old house. It was far in the country. A
> kind old man lived there. His name was the Professor. He had wild white
> hair. A kind woman named Mrs. Macready kept the house tidy. Lucy liked
> them both at once."

Nine sentences, eight of them "[Subject] [was/had/lived]…" in near-identical
5–9 word shape. This is the flattest sustained stretch in the draft and it
is the *first* thing a read-aloud audience hears. The names list and the
"His name was the Professor / He had wild white hair" pair are the
double-tap that drags most — the child already has "a kind old man" and
doesn't need name-then-hair in two equal beats.

**Reader cost:** Flow channel. A read-aloud audience forms its expectation
of the book's voice in the first fifteen seconds. Nine identical-shape
sentences tell the ear "this book will be a list," which undersells the
genuinely musical middle section (the wardrobe crossing and the lamp-post).
A sleepy opener costs attention the rest of the chapter has to win back.

**Fix direction:** Compress the house/Professor/Macready cluster into two
beats rather than five; let one be a sensory detail (the wild white hair is
good — keep it, but attach it to "the Professor" in one clause). The names
list can stay if the *next* sentence breaks shape.

## Minor

### M1 — "Lucy liked them both at once" — the opening's safety is told, not earned

Anchor (end of line 3). Lucy liking both the Professor and Mrs. Macready is
asserted as a summary, not shown through any behavior or detail. The T1
safe-home schema wants the reader to *feel* the house is safe; a label
("she liked them") does less safety-work than one concrete warm detail —
her hand in the Professor's, a biscuit, a smile that crinkles. Reader cost
is small because the chapter re-establishes safety later (the cupboard door
stays visible, "She could go home"), but the opening's safety settles as a
fact rather than a feeling.

### M2 — "Goodness gracious me!" loses the cliffhanger punctuation

Anchor (line 25). Source closing sentence (`ch-01.yaml`, CERTAIN):
*"Goodness gracious me!" exclaimed the Faun.* The draft renders it
correctly as the Faun's line but immediately resolves it into laughter and
friendship within the same beat. Per the brief this is an *intentional*
T1 reframing of the cliffhanger into a comic/friendly beat, so it is
*authorized* — flagged here only to note that the source's last-line
punctuation (the exclamation as the chapter's final sound) is traded away.
That is an acceptable trade for this register; just confirm it was the
intended choice and not an accidental soften. No reader cost if intended.

### M3 — "Edmund was grumpy and tired that first night" — the grumpiness has no consequence

Anchor (line 5). T1 permits "grumpy" as a labeled emotion
(`tier_1.yaml` permitted_emotions), and framing badness as a temporary
state is exactly the agency-externalization rule working correctly. But the
grumpiness is introduced and resolved in two sentences with no effect on
anything — it doesn't block the exploration, draw a reaction from Susan,
or pay off later. Reader cost is marginal (a child won't notice the
dangling thread), but it is a single-purpose beat: it only informs, it
doesn't also do character or pacing work. Either let it produce one small
behavior, or cut it — the opening doesn't need Edmund to be grumpy to
justify the exploration plan.

### M4 — "How funny!" and "looked ready for a party" are the voice at its best — protect them in revision

Anchor (lines 19, 21). Not a defect — a note to the writer. These two
moments are where the voice is most purely right for the register. "How
funny!" at the lamp-post is exactly the kind of child-voiced reaction that
keeps a 4–6-year-old inside Lucy's point of view, and "looked ready for a
party" is the strongest single line in the Faun description (it does
character work the five "He…" sentences don't). If the Significant findings
get fixed, make sure these two survive the revision intact.

## On the Faun reframing (focus area 4)

The reframing lands and does **not** feel contrived — with one caveat. The
mechanical sequence is sound: the Faun drops his parcels (canon), they go
"bump, bump, bump" (T1-appropriate onomatopoeia), he says his canonical
line, and Lucy laughs. This is a clean T1 translation of a startle into a
comic beat, and it correctly preserves the Faun as non-threatening
(`villain_treatment: always redeemable`; nightmare-prevention satisfied).

The one risk is **speed**. We go from "He jumped!" to "He was a friend" in
five sentences (lines 23–27), with the friendship *declared* rather than
*felt* — the same told-not-earned pattern as M1. The comic beat itself
works; the instant "He was a friend" declaration fast-forwards past the
moment the child reader needs to settle. Letting the Faun's kindness show
through one concrete action rather than the narrator labeling him "a
friend" would make the reframing feel *earned* rather than convenient.

## On fidelity (focus area 5)

Faithful. All ten essentials from `ch-01.yaml` are present and correctly
ordered: the four children, the Professor, Mrs. Macready, the rainy-day
exploration, the empty room with the single wardrobe, Lucy staying behind,
the sensory crossing, the snowy wood with the wardrobe door still visible,
the lamp-post, the Faun with parcels and umbrella dropping them on sight,
the canonical closing line. Nothing invented; nothing essential cut. The
source closing-sentence is preserved verbatim in dialogue (the only drift
is the immediate post-line laughter, which is the authorized reframing).
The "cupboard"/"wardrobe" lexical choice is a defensible young-reader
adaptation and not a fidelity problem; if there's a series-wide vocabulary
file, prefer consistency across chapters over either choice in isolation.

## Highest-priority fix

**S1 — the Faun's six "He…" sentences.** It is the chapter's biggest
reveal delivered in its flattest prose, it strikes the most-protected
reward channel (aesthetic) for this register, and it is the easiest fix on
the page — a shape-varied rewrite of one paragraph. Fix this and the Faun
introduction goes from "accurate list of attributes" to "a creature
actually steps into the snow."

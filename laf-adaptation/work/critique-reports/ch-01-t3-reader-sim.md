**Reader:** 9–11yo, concrete-operational, reading independently. Handles irony, richer vocabulary,
character nuance, atmosphere, a genuine cliffhanger. Transition tier. **Draft:** `work/drafts/ch-01-t3-v2.md`.

## Emotional arc (beat by beat)
Settled/amused (the narrator's "they do not come into the story much" wink) → comic wonder at the
Professor (Edmund stifling a laugh) → real-sibling squabble → creepy-house frisson (Peter's
stairs-and-passages boast makes the house enormous, so Lucy's unease has something to attach to) →
exploration momentum (restored "And after that… And then…" chain) → **wardrobe slow-burn wonder
(peak 1)** → the sensible safety rope (reads as characterization, not a crutch) → lamp-post
absurd-delight → **Faun reveal + "Christmas shopping" irony (peak 2)** → cliffhanger.

## Strongest engagement moments
1. **The wardrobe crossing** — fur → crunch → cold → prickly branches → snowy wood: gradual,
   dread-free wonder; the single best lean-in (read holding breath).
2. **The Faun reveal + "Christmas shopping"** — the narrator's irony lands; goat-man with parcels
   and umbrella is exactly the concrete-magical image this age loves, with a wink.
3. **The restored narrator asides** ("they do not come into the story much," "as I have said,"
   "very foolish to shut oneself into a wardrobe") — where Lewis's voice CHARMS not just informs.

## Does the "And then…" chain work or grate?
**It works.** For a 9–11yo the anaphoric "And after that… And then…" reads as the sensation of
wandering deeper into an inexhaustible house, not a list — it's short (one paragraph) and each
clause delivers a NEW concrete marvel (armour, green room + harp, steps, balcony, book-rooms), so
novelty outruns repetition. v2's restored chain is vindicated over v1's chopped waypoints. Keep it.

## Edmund — real kid, not villain?
**Yes.** The narrator explains him ("tired and pretending not to be tired, which always made him
bad-tempered") so the reader attributes his snark to a STATE they recognize; his lines are ordinary
sibling grumpiness. Reads as "the annoying brother" — affectionate recognition, no moral alarm.
Seeds his later arc honestly without pre-judging him.

## Stumbles / vocabulary (period diction, all context-carried, none blocking)
`blue-bottle` (a fly; "dead... on the window-sill" carries it — correctly kept at T3 vs T2's "fly"),
`muffler` (scarf), `wireless` (radio), `queer` (Lewis's "strange" sense), `inquisitive` (inferable,
a good stretch word). All appropriate to near-Lewis fidelity; flag only for a reluctant decoder.

## Cliffhanger
"Goodness gracious me!" — the strange goat-man startled by Lucy flips expectation and opens the
exact questions the reader wants (who is he, is he friendly, what is this snowy land?). Ends on
curiosity + comedy, no dread. Strong pull into Ch 2.

## Knowledge boundary
Faun met but NOT named (no Tumnus/cave/errand/Witch — correct for Ch-I-only). Reader leaves curious
about the Faun, NOT committed to "friend" — appropriate to this tier, which can carry the coming
ambiguity. Lucy = brave/curious (grounded in action); Edmund = prickly-but-real (cause named);
Narnia = wonder-with-a-chill, not yet safe/unsafe.

## Verdict: **Yes** — lands cleanly for an independent 9–11yo. Voice charms, the crossing wonders,
Edmund reads real, the house is invitingly eerie, the cliffhanger pulls forward.

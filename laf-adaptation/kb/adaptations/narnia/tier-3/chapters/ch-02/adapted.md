# What Lucy Found There

"Good evening," said Lucy. But the Faun was so busy picking up his parcels that at first he did not reply. When he had finished, he made her a little bow.

"Good evening, good evening," said the Faun. "Excuse me — I don't want to be nosy — but should I be right in thinking that you are a Daughter of Eve?"

"My name is Lucy," she said, not quite understanding him.

"But you are — forgive me — you are what they call a girl?" asked the Faun.

"Of course I'm a girl," said Lucy.

"You are, in fact, human?"

"Of course I'm human," said Lucy, still a little puzzled.

"To be sure, to be sure," said the Faun. "How silly of me! But I have never seen a Son of Adam or a Daughter of Eve before. I am delighted." Then he stopped, as if he had almost said something he had not meant to. "Delighted, delighted," he went on. "Allow me to introduce myself. My name is Tumnus."

"I am very pleased to meet you, Mr. Tumnus," said Lucy.

"And may I ask, O Lucy, Daughter of Eve," said Mr. Tumnus, "how you have come into Narnia?"

"Narnia? What's that?" said Lucy.

"This is the land of Narnia," said the Faun, "all that lies between the lamp-post and the great castle of Cair Paravel on the eastern sea. And you — you have come from the wild woods of the west?"

"I got in through the wardrobe in the spare room," said Lucy.

"Ah," said Mr. Tumnus rather sadly. "If only I had worked harder at geography when I was a little Faun. Then I should know all about those strange countries. It is too late now."

"But they aren't countries at all," said Lucy, almost laughing. "It's only just back there — at least, I think so. It's summer there."

"Meanwhile," said Mr. Tumnus, "it is winter here in Narnia, and has been for ever so long. We shall both catch cold if we stand talking in the snow. Daughter of Eve from the far land of Spare Oom, how would it be if you came and had tea with me?"

"Thank you very much, Mr. Tumnus," said Lucy. "But I was wondering whether I ought to be getting back."

"It's only just round the corner," said the Faun, "and there'll be a roaring fire, and toast, and sardines, and cake."

"Well, it's very kind of you," said Lucy. "But I shan't be able to stay long."

So Lucy took his arm, and he held the umbrella over them both. They set off through the wood as if they had known one another all their lives.

Before long they came to a place where the ground grew rough, with rocks and little hills. At the bottom of one small valley Mr. Tumnus turned aside, as though he meant to walk straight into a large rock. But at the last moment Lucy saw he was leading her into the mouth of a cave. Inside, she blinked in the light of a wood fire. Mr. Tumnus lit a lamp, put a kettle on, and said, "Now we shan't be long."

Lucy thought she had never been in a nicer place. It was a small, dry, clean cave of reddish stone. There was a carpet on the floor and two little chairs. "One for me and one for a friend," said Mr. Tumnus. There was a table, a dresser, and a mantelpiece over the fire. Above it hung a picture of an old Faun with a grey beard. On one wall was a shelf of books, with titles like *The Life and Letters of Silenus* and *Is Man a Myth?*

And it was a wonderful tea. There was a lightly boiled egg for each of them, and sardines on toast, and buttered toast, and toast with honey, and a sugar-topped cake. When Lucy was tired of eating, the Faun began to talk. He had wonderful tales to tell of life in the forest. He told of the midnight dances, when the Nymphs of the wells and the Dryads of the trees came out to dance with the Fauns. He told of long hunts after the milk-white Stag, who could grant your wishes if you caught him. He told of feasting and treasure-seeking with the Red Dwarfs, in deep mines far below the forest floor. And he told of summer, when the woods were green and old Silenus came to visit, and the whole forest kept festival for weeks on end.

"Not that it isn't always winter now," he added gloomily. Then, to cheer himself, he took a strange little flute from the dresser and began to play. The tune made Lucy want to cry and laugh and dance and fall asleep, all at the same time. It must have been hours later when she shook herself and said:

"Oh, Mr. Tumnus — I'm so sorry to stop you, and I do love that tune — but really, I must go home. I only meant to stay a few minutes."

"It's no good now, you know," said the Faun, laying down his flute and shaking his head at her sorrowfully.

"No good? What do you mean?" said Lucy, jumping up, suddenly rather frightened. "I've got to go home at once. The others will be wondering where I am." But then she saw that the Faun's eyes had filled with tears, and soon he had covered his face with his hands and begun to sob as though his heart would break.

"Mr. Tumnus! Mr. Tumnus!" said Lucy, in great distress. "Don't! What is the matter?" She went over and put her arms round him and lent him her handkerchief, but still he wept.

"Mr. Tumnus," she said at last. "Do stop. Tell me — whatever are you crying about?"

"Oh," sobbed Mr. Tumnus, "I'm crying because I'm such a bad Faun."

"I don't think you're a bad Faun at all," said Lucy. "I think you are a very good Faun. You are the nicest Faun I've ever met."

"You wouldn't say that if you knew," he replied. "No, I'm a bad Faun. I don't suppose there ever was a worse one since the beginning of the world."

"But what have you done?" asked Lucy.

"My old father, now — that's his picture over the mantelpiece — he would never have done a thing like this."

"A thing like what?" said Lucy.

"Like what I've done," said the Faun. "Taken service under the White Witch. I'm in the pay of the White Witch."

"The White Witch? Who is she?"

"Why, she is the one who has all Narnia under her thumb. It's she who makes it always winter — always winter, and never Christmas. Think of that!"

"How awful!" said Lucy. "But what does she pay you to do?"

"That's the worst of it," said Mr. Tumnus with a deep groan. "I'm a kidnapper for her, that's what I am. I had orders that if ever I found a Daughter of Eve in the wood, I was to catch her and hand her over to the Witch. And you are the first I have ever met. I meant to wait until you were asleep, and then go and tell her."

"Oh, but you won't, Mr. Tumnus," said Lucy, turning rather pale. "You won't, will you? You really mustn't."

"And if I don't," he said, beginning to cry again, "she is sure to find out. She has cruel punishments for a Faun who disobeys her. She might even turn me to stone, so that I would be nothing but a statue of a Faun in her house. And there I would stand until the four thrones at Cair Paravel are filled — and goodness knows when that will be."

"I'm very sorry, Mr. Tumnus," said Lucy. "But please let me go home."

"Of course I will," said the Faun. "Of course I've got to. I see that now. I hadn't known what Humans were like before I met you. Now that I know you, I could never hand you over to the Witch. But we must be off at once. I'll see you back to the lamp-post. You can find your own way from there, back to Spare Oom and War Drobe?"

"I'm sure I can," said Lucy.

"We must go as quietly as we can," said Mr. Tumnus. "The whole wood is full of her spies. Even some of the trees are on her side."

They left the tea things on the table, and Mr. Tumnus put up his umbrella again and gave Lucy his arm, and they went out into the snow. The journey back was nothing like the journey to the cave. They stole along as quickly and quietly as they could, without a word, and Mr. Tumnus kept to the darkest places. Lucy was very relieved when they reached the lamp-post again.

"Do you know your way from here, Daughter of Eve?" asked Tumnus.

Lucy looked hard between the trees and could just make out, in the distance, a patch of light that looked like daylight. "Yes," she said, "I can see the wardrobe door."

"Then be off home as quick as you can," said the Faun, "and — can you ever forgive me for what I meant to do?"

"Why, of course I can," said Lucy, shaking him warmly by the hand. "And I do hope you won't get into dreadful trouble on my account."

"Farewell, Daughter of Eve," said he. "Perhaps I may keep the handkerchief?"

"Rather!" said Lucy, and then she ran toward the far-off patch of daylight as fast as her legs would carry her. Soon, instead of rough branches brushing past her, she felt coats; and instead of crunching snow beneath her feet, she felt wooden boards. All at once she found herself jumping out of the wardrobe into the same empty room from which the whole adventure had started.

"I'm here," she shouted. "I'm here. I've come back, I'm all right."

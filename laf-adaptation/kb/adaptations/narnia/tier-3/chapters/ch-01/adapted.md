# Lucy Looks into a Wardrobe

Once there were four children whose names were Peter, Susan, Edmund, and Lucy. This story is about something that happened to them during the war. They were sent away from London because of the air-raids. They went to the house of an old Professor, who lived in the heart of the country. It stood ten miles from the nearest railway station and two miles from the nearest post office.

He had no wife, and he lived in a very large house with a housekeeper called Mrs. Macready and three servants. Their names were Ivy, Margaret, and Betty, but they do not come into the story much. He himself was a very old man with shaggy white hair. The hair grew over most of his face as well as on his head. The children liked him almost at once.

But on the first evening he came out to meet them at the front door. He looked so odd that Lucy, the youngest, was a little afraid of him. Edmund, the next youngest, wanted to laugh. He kept pretending to blow his nose, so nobody would see.

That night the boys came into the girls' room, and they all talked it over.

"We've fallen on our feet and no mistake," said Peter. "This is going to be perfectly splendid. That old chap will let us do anything we like."

"I think he's an old dear," said Susan.

"Oh, come off it!" said Edmund. He was tired and pretending not to be tired, which always made him bad-tempered. "Don't go on talking like that."

"Like what?" said Susan. "And anyway, it's time you were in bed."

"Trying to talk like Mother," said Edmund. "Who are you to say when I go to bed? Go to bed yourself."

"Hadn't we all better go to bed?" said Lucy. "There's sure to be a row if we're heard."

"No there won't," said Peter. "This is the sort of house where no one minds what we do. Anyway, they won't hear us. It's about ten minutes' walk down to the dining room, with any amount of stairs and passages in between."

"What's that noise?" said Lucy suddenly. It was a far larger house than she had ever slept in. She thought of all those long passages, and the rows of doors leading into empty rooms. It made her feel a little creepy.

"It's only a bird, silly," said Edmund.

"It's an owl," said Peter. "This is going to be a wonderful place for birds. Let's explore to-morrow. You might find anything in a place like this. There might be eagles. There might be stags. There'll be hawks."

"Badgers!" said Lucy.

"Snakes!" said Edmund.

"Foxes!" said Susan.

But next morning brought a steady rain, thick and grey. When you looked out of the window, you could see neither the mountains nor the woods.

"Of course it would be raining," said Edmund. They had just finished breakfast with the Professor. Now they were upstairs, in the long low room he had set apart for them.

"Do stop grumbling, Ed," said Susan. "Ten to one it'll clear up in an hour. And in the meantime we're pretty well off. There's a wireless and lots of books."

"Not for me," said Peter. "I'm going to explore in the house."

Everyone agreed, and that was how the adventures began. It was the sort of house you never seem to come to the end of. It was full of unexpected places.

The first few doors led only into spare bedrooms, just as everyone had expected. But soon they came to a very long room full of pictures, and there they found a suit of armour. And after that was a room all hung with green, with a harp in one corner. And then came three steps down, and five steps up, and a little upstairs hall. It had a door that led onto a balcony. And then a whole row of rooms, all lined with books. Most of the books were very old, and some were bigger than a Bible in a church.

And shortly after that they looked into a room that was quite empty. It held only one big wardrobe, the sort with a looking-glass in the door. There was nothing else in the room at all, except a dead blue-bottle on the window-sill.

"Nothing there!" said Peter, and they all trooped out again — all except Lucy. She stayed behind, because she thought it worth while trying the door of the wardrobe. She felt almost sure it would be locked. But to her surprise it opened quite easily, and two moth-balls dropped out.

Looking into the inside, she saw several coats hanging up, mostly long fur coats. There was nothing Lucy liked so much as the smell and feel of fur. She immediately stepped into the wardrobe, got in among the coats, and rubbed her face against them. She left the door open, of course, because she knew it is very foolish to shut oneself into a wardrobe.

Soon she went further in, and found a second row of coats hanging up behind the first. It was almost quite dark now. She kept her arms stretched out in front, so as not to bump the back of the wardrobe. She took a step further, then two or three steps more. Always she expected to feel woodwork against the tips of her fingers. But she could not feel it.

"This must be a simply enormous wardrobe!" thought Lucy. She went still further in, pushing the soft folds of the coats aside to make room for herself. Then she noticed something crunching under her feet. "I wonder, is that more moth-balls?" she thought. She stooped down to feel it with her hands. But instead of hard, smooth wood, she felt something soft and powdery and extremely cold.

"This is very queer," she said, and went on a step or two.

Next moment, what rubbed against her face was no longer soft fur. It was something hard and rough and even prickly. "Why, it is just like branches of trees!" exclaimed Lucy. And then she saw a light ahead of her. It was not a few inches away, where the back of the wardrobe ought to be. It was a long way off, and something cold and soft was falling on her.

A moment later she was standing in the middle of a wood at night-time. There was snow under her feet, and snowflakes falling through the air.

Lucy felt a little frightened, but she felt inquisitive and excited as well. She looked back over her shoulder. There, between the dark tree-trunks, she could still see the open doorway of the wardrobe. She could even catch a glimpse of the empty room she had come from. It still seemed to be daylight there.

"I can always get back if anything goes wrong," thought Lucy. She began to walk forward, crunch-crunch, over the snow and through the wood, towards the far-off light.

In about ten minutes she reached it and found that it was a lamp-post. She stood looking at it, and wondering what to do next. Why was there a lamp-post in the middle of a wood? Then she heard a pitter-patter of feet coming towards her. And soon after, a very strange person stepped out of the trees into the light of the lamp-post.

He was only a little taller than Lucy herself. He carried an umbrella over his head, white with snow. From the waist up he was like a man. But his legs were shaped like a goat's, with glossy black hair, and instead of feet he had goat's hoofs. He had a tail too, though Lucy did not notice it at first. It was neatly caught up over his arm to keep it from trailing in the snow.

He had a red woollen muffler round his neck, and his skin was rather reddish. His face was strange but pleasant, with a short pointed beard and curly hair. Out of the hair stuck two horns, one on each side of his forehead. One hand, as I have said, held the umbrella. In the other arm he carried several brown paper parcels. What with the parcels and the snow, he looked just as if he had been doing his Christmas shopping.

He was a Faun. And when he saw Lucy he gave such a start of surprise that he dropped all his parcels.

"Goodness gracious me!" exclaimed the Faun.

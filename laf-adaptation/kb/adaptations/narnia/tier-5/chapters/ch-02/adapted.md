# Chapter II — What Lucy Found There — Tier 5 (Preservation)

## Preservation declaration

At Tier 5 (ages 15–17), this chapter is **preserved with no transformation**. Tier 5's paradigm is "Minimal transformation — preserve author intent; adaptation means accessibility, not sanitization" (`kb/tiers/tier_5.yaml`). Chapter II carries genuine moral weight — a charming host confesses he is in the pay of the White Witch, that he was ordered to kidnap Lucy and had meant to betray her, and that he now repents at real personal risk — and that weight is exactly what a competent 15–17 reader is equipped to meet. The correct T5 outcome is therefore **zero transformation of the prose**.

The **canonical text is C. S. Lewis's original**, read as-is from the source at `laf-adaptation/source/narnia/ch-02.txt`. This record does not reproduce, rewrite, condense, or paraphrase that text; the source file remains the authoritative reading copy. What follows is the supplementary accessibility scaffolding that `tier_5.yaml`'s `supplementary_approach` prescribes — context notes and discussion questions — never a substitute text.

Per `tier_5.yaml`, the following are **preserved exactly as Lewis wrote them**, with no intervention:

- **Agency-externalization is forbidden at T5** (`transformation_rules.agency_externalization: forbidden` — "Patronizing; undermines character complexity"). Mr. Tumnus owns his choice: he is genuinely in the Witch's pay, genuinely planned the betrayal, and genuinely repents. His moral crisis is not softened, reassigned, or explained away as an external state.
- **The confession in full** — Tumnus naming himself "a kidnapper," admitting "I'm doing it now, this very moment," and revealing that Lucy is the child he was luring — is preserved intact, including Lucy's fright ("turning very white").
- **The Witch's threatened punishments** — the tail cut off, the horns sawn off, the beard plucked, the hoofs transformed, and the turning-to-stone until the four thrones at Cair Paravel are filled — stand as written. No mutilation detail is trimmed.
- **The White Witch's tyranny** — that she holds all Narnia under her thumb and makes it "always winter and never Christmas" — is kept as the chapter's grounding of the larger evil.
- **The repentance and the meaning** — "I hadn't known what Humans were like before I met you … not now that I know you" — is preserved as the chapter's turning point: friendship, not force, overturns a betrayal in motion.

The chapter ends on **Lucy's safe return** through the wardrobe into the same empty room, calling out that she is back and all right — preserved as the closing beat, closing the first excursion into Narnia.

## Accessibility notes

Per T5's sole permitted intervention (`when_to_intervene`: historical context and now-obscure cultural references; `archaic_handling: footnote_optional`), the following are brief **context glosses about the source** for a modern 15–17 reader. They are notes *about* the text, not changes *to* it.

- **"Daughter of Eve" / "Son of Adam"** — Lewis's Narnian idiom for a human female / male, drawn from the Genesis figures Adam and Eve. Fauns and other Narnian creatures use these titles for humans throughout the book; here Tumnus is confirming that Lucy is a human being.
- **Faun** — a creature from Roman mythology, part human and part goat (a counterpart to the Greek satyr), associated with the wild woods, music, and revelry. Lewis's Mr. Tumnus keeps the mythological form — hoofs, horns, a straw flute — while giving him a wholly domestic, English cosiness (umbrella, tea, a book-lined cave).
- **Silenus and Bacchus** — figures from Greek/Roman myth. Bacchus (Dionysus) is the god of wine and ecstatic festival; Silenus is his elderly, perpetually tipsy companion, usually shown riding a donkey. Tumnus's tale of summers "when the streams would run with wine" and "the whole forest would give itself up to jollification" evokes these classical revels.
- **Nymphs and Dryads** — nature-spirits of classical myth: Nymphs inhabit springs and wells (water); Dryads inhabit trees (here, "the Dryads who lived in the trees"). Their coming out to dance with the Fauns is part of the older, freer Narnia the Witch's winter has suppressed.
- **"Spare Oom" and "War Drobe"** — Tumnus's charming mishearing of Lucy's "the spare room" and "the wardrobe" as exotic foreign place-names (the "far land of Spare Oom," the "bright city of War Drobe"). A small joke that also underlines how alien the ordinary human world is to him.
- **The milk-white Stag** — a wish-granting white stag of legend; Lewis returns to it at the very end of the book, so a first-time reader may note it here as a planted seed.
- **Cair Paravel and "the four thrones"** — the great castle on the eastern sea and its four empty thrones. Tumnus's fear that he might stay a statue "until the four thrones at Cair Paravel are filled" is the chapter's first, oblique hint of the prophecy that four humans will one day end the Witch's reign — a promise the reader is not yet in a position to decode.
- **The book titles** — *The Life and Letters of Silenus*, *Nymphs and Their Ways*, *Men, Monks and Gamekeepers; a Study in Popular Legend*, *Is Man a Myth?* — mock-scholarly titles that reverse our world's frame: to a Narnian Faun, *humans* are the mythical creatures whose existence is debated. A dry joke that rewards a reader who catches the inversion.

## Discussion questions

Thematic and close-reading prompts appropriate for ages 15–17 (`supplementary_approach.recommended`: "Discussion questions for themes"):

1. **Hospitality as a trap — and its undoing.** Every warm detail of the tea scene — the fire, the cake, the tales, the flute — is, we learn, part of a plan to lull Lucy asleep and betray her. On a second reading, how does that knowledge change the cave sequence? Does Lewis want us to feel the hospitality as sinister, or as genuine warmth that becomes real to Tumnus even as he means to abuse it?

2. **What actually changes Tumnus's mind.** Tumnus does not repent because Lucy argues him out of it, nor because he fears getting caught — he explicitly expects to be punished either way. His stated reason is "I hadn't known what Humans were like before I met you." What is Lewis claiming here about how moral change happens? Why might *knowing a particular person* do what abstract principle could not?

3. **Fear on both sides.** Lucy "turns very white" when she realises she is the intended victim; Tumnus is terrified of the Witch's punishments. Both are afraid, and both choose rightly anyway. How does the chapter distinguish courage from fearlessness, and what does it suggest about acting well while still being afraid?

4. **The comedy and the dread, held together.** The chapter is by turns very funny ("Spare Oom," the mock book titles, the mishearings) and genuinely dark (a confessed kidnapper, threats of mutilation and petrification). How does Lewis manage the tonal swing without either the comedy trivialising the danger or the danger souring the charm? What does that blend do to the reader's sense of Narnia as a place?

5. **A miniature of the whole book.** A sinner confesses a betrayal, repents at real cost, and is freely forgiven by the one he wronged. Having read (or knowing) the rest of the novel, how does this small early episode rhyme with the larger arc of betrayal, sacrifice, and forgiveness to come? Why might Lewis place such a model of confession-and-forgiveness at the very threshold of the story?

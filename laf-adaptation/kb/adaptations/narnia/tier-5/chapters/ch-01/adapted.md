## Accessibility notes

Per T5's sole permitted intervention (`when_to_intervene`: historical context and now-obscure cultural references; `archaic_handling: footnote_optional`), the following are brief **context glosses about the source** for a modern 15–17 reader. They are notes *about* the text, not changes *to* it.

- **Wireless** — a radio set. In 1940s Britain "the wireless" was the ordinary word for a home radio, the main source of news and entertainment.
- **Air-raids / wartime evacuation** — during the Second World War, German bombing raids ("the Blitz") threatened London and other cities, so large numbers of children were sent ("evacuated") from the cities to safer homes in the countryside. This is why the four children are staying with the Professor.
- **Blue-bottle** — a large, common fly with a metallic blue body (a blowfly). The dead one on the window-sill is simply a bit of realistic, dusty-old-house detail.
- **Moth-balls** — small balls of a strong-smelling substance kept in wardrobes and drawers to protect stored clothes (like the fur coats) from clothes-moths.
- **Suit of armour** — a full set of protective metal plate worn by a medieval knight, here displayed as a decorative antique in the grand old house.

## Discussion questions

Thematic and close-reading prompts appropriate for ages 15–17 (`supplementary_approach.recommended`: "Discussion questions for themes"):

1. **The mundane and the marvellous.** Lewis grounds the chapter in a deliberately ordinary, even dreary frame — an evacuation, a rainy day, an empty room, a wardrobe full of coats — before rupturing it with wonder. Why might he build the magical crossing out of such unremarkable materials? What does the contrast do to the reader's sense of where wonder can be found?

2. **Lucy as the one who crosses.** Of the four children, it is the youngest who lingers, opens the wardrobe, and steps a little further into the dark. How does Lewis characterise Lucy through her curiosity — the impulse to feel and smell the fur, to keep going deeper — and what does it suggest that inquisitiveness, rather than boldness or cleverness, is what opens the hidden world?

3. **The threshold and the lamp-post.** Consider the two boundary images: the open wardrobe door Lucy keeps in sight behind her, and the lamp-post standing incongruously among the snowy trees. What makes a lamp-post — a piece of ordinary human street furniture — such a striking thing to plant in the middle of a wild wood? How do both images work as thresholds between two worlds?

4. **Narrative voice.** The chapter is told by a narrator who addresses the reader directly and comments on the characters (for instance, on Edmund's temper, or on the folly of shutting oneself into a wardrobe). How does this warm, slightly knowing storyteller's voice shape the tone, and how might it read differently to a 15–17 audience than to the younger children the book is often given to?

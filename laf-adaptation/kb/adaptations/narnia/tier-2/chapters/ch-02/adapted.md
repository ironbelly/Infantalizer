# Tea with Mr. Tumnus

"Good evening," said Lucy politely.

The Faun was busy gathering his parcels. When he had finished, he gave her a little bow.

"Good evening," he said. "Excuse me for asking. But are you really a girl? A human girl?"

"Of course I am," said Lucy. "My name is Lucy."

"How wonderful! I have never met a human before," said the Faun. "Allow me to introduce myself. My name is Tumnus."

"I am very pleased to meet you, Mr. Tumnus," said Lucy.

"And how did you come into Narnia?" asked Mr. Tumnus.

"Narnia? What is that?" said Lucy.

"This is the land of Narnia," said the Faun. "It runs from this lamp-post to a great castle on the sea. But how did you get here?"

"I came in through a wardrobe in the spare room," said Lucy.

Mr. Tumnus did not quite understand. He thought she came from a far country called Spare Oom. But he was too polite to say so.

"It is winter here, and very cold," he said. "We shall both catch a chill. Would you like to come and have tea with me?"

"Thank you very much," said Lucy. "But I mustn't stay long."

"It is only round the corner," said the Faun. "There will be a warm fire, and toast, and cake."

So Lucy took his arm. He held the umbrella over them both. Off they went through the snow.

Soon they came to a little cave among the rocks. Inside it was snug and warm. A wood fire crackled in the hearth. There was a soft carpet, two little chairs, and a shelf of books. Over the fireplace hung a picture of an old Faun with a grey beard.

"That is my father," said Mr. Tumnus.

Then he set out a splendid tea. There was a boiled egg for each of them. There was sardines on toast, buttered toast, and toast with honey. Best of all, there was a sugar-topped cake. When Lucy could eat no more, the Faun began to talk.

He told her wonderful tales of the forest. He told of midnight dances in the moonlight. The tree-spirits and water-spirits came out to dance with the Fauns. He told of long hunts after the milk-white Stag, who could grant your wishes. He told of feasts underground with the merry Red Dwarfs. And he told of summer, when the forest kept festival for weeks.

"Though of course it is always winter now," he added sadly.

To cheer himself up, he took out a little flute. He began to play. The tune was so strange and sweet. It made Lucy want to laugh and cry and dance all at once. She listened for what felt like hours.

At last she gave herself a shake. "Oh, Mr. Tumnus," she said. "I am so sorry to stop you. I do love that tune. But really, I must go home now."

The Faun laid down his flute and shook his head. Then his eyes filled with tears. A moment later he was weeping into his hands.

"Mr. Tumnus! Whatever is the matter?" cried Lucy. "Are you not well? Please tell me what is wrong."

"Oh," he sobbed, "I am crying because I am such a bad Faun. I have done a mean thing."

"I don't think you are bad at all," said Lucy. "I think you are the nicest Faun I ever met."

"You would not say so if you knew," he said. "You see, I work for the White Witch."

"The White Witch? Who is she?" asked Lucy.

"She holds all Narnia under her spell," said Mr. Tumnus. "It is she who makes it always winter, and never Christmas. Think of that!"

"How mean of her!" said Lucy. "But what does she pay you to do?"

"That is the worst part," said the Faun. "She told me to watch for any human in the wood. If I met one, I was to bring them to her. And you are the first human I ever met. I planned to keep you here until you fell asleep. Then I would fetch the Witch."

Lucy jumped up, a little frightened. "Oh, Mr. Tumnus! You won't really do that, will you?"

"No," said the Faun firmly. "I thought I could. But I couldn't. Not now that I know you. You have been so kind, and we are friends. I could never hand a friend to the Witch."

Lucy felt very glad. "Then it is all right," she said gently. "You changed your mind. That is the brave thing to do."

"But we must be quick," said Mr. Tumnus. "The whole wood is full of the Witch's spies. I will see you safely back to the lamp-post."

They left the tea things on the table. Once more the Faun put up his umbrella and gave Lucy his arm. This time they did not talk. They hurried along, keeping to the shadows, until they reached the lamp-post again.

"Do you know your way from here?" asked Mr. Tumnus.

Lucy looked hard between the trees. Far off she could see a patch of light, like daylight. "Yes," she said. "I can see the wardrobe door."

"Then hurry home as fast as you can," said the Faun. "And — can you ever forgive me for what I meant to do?"

"Why, of course I can," said Lucy, shaking his hand warmly. "And I do hope you won't get into trouble because of me."

"Goodbye, Lucy," he said. "Might I keep your handkerchief?"

"Of course!" said Lucy. Then she ran toward the far-off daylight as fast as her legs would go.

The snow gave way to wooden boards. The branches turned into soft coats. And all at once she tumbled out of the wardrobe. She was in the same empty room where the whole adventure had begun.

"I'm here!" she shouted. "I'm here. I've come back. I'm all right!"
